                                                                                           Abstract
                                                       The construction of non-Abelian Euclidean Yang–Mills theories in dimension
                                                   four, as scaling limits of lattice Yang–Mills theories or otherwise, is a central open
                                                   question of mathematical physics. This paper takes the following small step to-
                                                   wards this goal. In any dimension d ≥ 2, we construct a scaling limit of SU(2)
                                                   lattice Yang–Mills theory coupled to a Higgs field (under the degenerate potential)
                                                   transforming in the fundamental representation of SU(2). After unitary gauge fixing
                                                   and taking the lattice spacing ε → 0, and simultaneously taking the gauge coupling
                                                   constant g → 0 and the Higgs length α → ∞ in such a manner that αg is always
                                                   equal to cε for some fixed c and g = O(ε50d ), a stereographic projection of the
                                                   gauge field is shown to converge to a massive Gaussian field. This gives the first
                                                   construction of a scaling limit of a non-Abelian lattice Yang–Mills theory in a di-
                                                   mension higher than two, as well as the first rigorous proof of mass generation by
                                                   the Higgs mechanism in such a theory. Analogous results are proved for U(1) the-
                                                   ory as well. The question of constructing a non-Gaussian scaling limit remains open.

                                                   Key words and phrases. Higgs mechanism, Yang–Mills theory, mass gap.

1    Lattice Yang–Mills theory with a Higgs field
Quantum Yang–Mills theories are the building blocks of the Standard Model of particle
physics. While these are quantum theories in Minkowski space, there are analogues
in Euclidean space, known as Euclidean Yang–Mills theories. Physicists believe that
calculations in the Euclidean theories can be carried over to the quantum theories. The
constructive QFT program aims to justify this mathematically, although it is not yet
well-developed for Yang–Mills theories [28, 34].
    Euclidean Yang–Mills theories are supposed to be classical statistical mechanical
models of quantum Yang–Mills theories. However, we do not know how to construct
even these classical models in a rigorous way in dimensions higher than two. Lattice
Yang–Mills theories, also known as lattice gauge theories, are discretized versions of
Euclidean Yang–Mills theories that are mathematically well-defined.

1.1   Definition
Let G be a compact Lie group, called the gauge group of the theory. Let d ≥ 2 and
L ≥ 1 be two integers, and let Λ := {−L, . . . , L}d . Our theory will be defined on Λ, and
eventually L will be taken to infinity. Throughout this paper, we will consider theories
on Λ with periodic boundary condition, meaning that opposite faces of Λ are identified
to be the same, giving Λ the structure of a torus.
    Let E denote the set of edges of Λ that are oriented in the positive direction (i.e.,
edges like e = (x, y) where x ≺ y in the lexicographic ordering). Note that this definition
makes sense even after identifying opposite faces, because two edges that are identified
to be the same are either both positively oriented, or both negatively oriented. The
space of gauge field configurations of the theory is Σ := GE . That is, a gauge field
configuration is an assignment of group elements to edges. Let us denote configurations
as U = (Ue )e∈E . If e = (x, y) ∈ E and e′ = (y, x) is the negatively oriented version of e,
then we define Ue′ := Ue−1 .
    A plaquette in Λ is a square bounded by four edges. Given a configuration U and a
plaquette p bounded by four directed edges e1 , e2 , e3 , e4 joined end-to-end (see Figure 1),
we define
                                   Up := Ue1 Ue2 Ue3 Ue4 .
Note that there is an ambiguity here about the choice of the first edge and the direction
of traversal, but that would be immaterial because we will only deal with the quantities
that are not affected by these choices.
    Let K be a positive integer. A Higgs field ϕ is a map from Λ into CK . Let Γ := (CK )Λ
denote the set of all Higgs fields (where again, vertices on opposite faces are identified
to be the same). The space of configurations for our lattice gauge theory coupled to a
Higgs field is Σ × Γ.
    Choose a number g > 0, called the gauge coupling constant, and a number α > 0,
called the Higgs length. Let ρ be a finite dimensional representation of G, and χρ be
its character. Let σ be a representation of G in CK , which is unitary with respect to
the usual inner product on CK . Let W : [0, ∞) → R be a function, called the Higgs
potential, that grows faster than quadratic at infinity. The Yang–Mills action is defined

1.2   Gauge transforms and gauge fixing
Let Θ := GΛ . That is, an element θ ∈ Θ assigns group elements to vertices. Elements
of Θ are called gauge transforms. Gauge transforms form a group under pointwise
multiplication, which acts on Σ × Γ as follows. For (U, ϕ) ∈ Σ × Γ and θ ∈ Θ, we define
(V, ψ) := θ(U, ϕ) as
                             Ve = θx Ue θy−1 , ψx = σ(θx )ϕx ,
where e = (x, y). Using the unitarity of σ, it is not hard to verify that S is gauge
invariant, that is, S(θ(U, ϕ)) = S(U, ϕ). Consequently, the theory has gauge symmetry,
meaning that the probability measure µ is preserved by gauge transforms.
    Any procedure that selects exactly one element from each orbit of Θ is called a
method for fixing a gauge. To understand the behavior of a lattice Yang–Mills theory,
it suffices to understand a gauge fixed version of the theory, because physically relevant
observables are required to be invariant under gauge transforms.

2     Random distributional 1-forms
The main results of this paper are in the next section. The results show that the U(1) and
the SU(2) lattice Yang–Mills theories defined in Subsections 1.3 and 1.4 converge, under
appropriate scalings of the lattice, to random distributional 1-forms. In this section, we
define these objects and describe some of their essential properties.

2.1   Definition
Recall that a function f : Rd → R is said to be rapidly decaying if for any α > 0, there
is some C such that |f (x)| ≤ C(1 + ∥x∥)−α for all x ∈ Rd . Let S(Rd ) denote the space
of real-valued Schwartz functions on Rd , i.e., infinitely differentiable functions f such
that f and all its derivatives of all orders are rapidly decaying functions. We define a
Schwartzian 1-form on Rd as a d-tuple f = (f1 , . . . , fd ) of real-valued Schwartz functions
on Rd . As a matter of notation, we will write f = f1 dx1 + · · · + fd dxd . Let A(Rd ) denote
the space of Schwartzian 1-forms on Rd .
    Let (Ω, F, P) be a probability space. We define a random 1-form on Rd to be a
measurable map X : Ω × Rd → Rd that satisfies, for any compact set K ⊆ Rd , any
1 ≤ i ≤ d, and almost every ω ∈ Ω,

3     Main results
We are now ready to state the two main results of the paper. The first result establishes
the limit of U(1) Yang–Mills–Higgs theory under a certain scaling of the gauge coupling
constant and the Higgs length. The second result gives the analogous statement for
SU(2) theory.

3.1   A scaling limit of U(1) Yang–Mills–Higgs theory
Recall the gauge field V defined in Subsection 1.3, obtained by unitary gauge fixing of
the original theory, but in infinite volume. That is, consider any infinite volume Gibbs
measure obtained by taking a weak limit of the models on Λ under periodic boundary
condition, as L → ∞. The result stated below does not depend on the choice of the
infinite volume measure, as long as it is a weak limit of finite volume measures under
periodic boundary conditions.
    For each edge e in Zd , Ve is an element of U(1), which can be viewed as the unit
circle S 1 ⊆ R2 . We obtain a projection of Ve on the real line as follows. For each n,

3.3   Comparison with prior work
Scaling limits of U(1) theory coupled to a Higgs field have appeared in the prior literature,
mostly in the late 70s and early 80s. Some notable contributions are due to Brydges,
Fröhlich, and Seiler [12], Balaban, Imbrie, Jaffe, and Brydges [8] and Kennedy and King
[35], who established mass generation by the Higgs mechanism in various versions of
the U(1) Yang–Mills–Higgs model on a lattice in d = 2 and d = 3. In d = 3, Balaban
[2, 3, 4, 5] proved ultraviolet stability of U(1) theory coupled to a Higgs field, which
is the first step towards the construction of a continuum limit. For a survey of these
and other results, see Borgs and Nill [10]. The closest cousins of Theorem 3.1 in the
literature are the results of Gross [31] and Driver [22], who proved convergence of the
curvature forms of pure U(1) theory (i.e., without a Higgs field) to massless versions of
the Euclidean Proca field in d = 3 and d = 4, respectively.
    In a different setup, Dimock [20, 21] has proved ultraviolet stability of U(1) theory
coupled to fermions in d = 3, using Balaban’s approach to renormalization. This is the
theory of quantum electrodynamics in d = 3. A key difference between these works and
Theorem 3.1 is that it is the first result that obtains a massive field in the scaling limit,
in a dimension greater than two. All of the above results are either in d = 2, or obtain
a massless field in d ≥ 3.
    Similarly, Theorem 3.2 is the first construction of a massive scaling limit of a non-
Abelian lattice Yang–Mills theory in dimensions bigger than two. For prior progress on
constructions of scaling limits of non-Abelian Yang–Mills theories in d ≥ 3, see the works
of Balaban [6, 7] and Magnen, Rivasseau, and Sénéor [37]. There has been a resurgence of
interest in this topic in recent years, with contributions from the probability community.
An approach to constructing Euclidean Yang–Mills–Higgs theories via rigorous stochastic
quantization was initiated by Shen [41] and Chandra, Chevyrev, Hairer, and Shen [15,
16]. For a survey of this rapidly evolving area, see [18]. For a different approach based on
regularization by Yang–Mills heat flows, see Gross [32] and Cao and Chatterjee [13, 14].
None of these approaches have yet reached their ultimate goal of constructing non-
Abelian Euclidean Yang–Mills theories in d ≥ 3, but the case looks promising.
    In d = 2, on the other hand, we now have a fairly complete understanding of scaling
limits of non-Abelian Yang–Mills theories, due to the contributions of many authors,
such as Driver [23], Gross, King, and Sengupta [33], Fine [24], Sengupta [40], Lévy [36]
and Chevyrev [17]. More recently, Chevyrev and Shen [19] have characterized the 2D
Yang–Mills measure as the unique invariant measure of the Langevin dynamic; this is
important for universality of scaling limits.
    However, the construction of Yang–Mills–Higgs theories in the continuum remains

                                             13
open even in 2D. The most important recent progress in this direction is the work
of Bringmann and Cao [11], which proves global well-posedness of the Abelian Yang–
Mills–Higgs heat flow in 2D, essentially giving a dynamical construction of Abelian
Yang–Mills–Higgs theory in 2D.
    Two recent works that are analogous to Theorem 3.2 in that they obtained Gaussian
limits of discrete models with non-Abelian symmetries are those of Aru, Garban, and
Sepúlveda [1] and Giuliani and Ott [27]. These papers look at the spin O(N ) models in
d = 2 and obtain Gaussian scaling limits. Unlike Theorem 3.2, however, the scaling limits
obtained in [1, 27] are non-massive. The proof sketch provided in the next subsection
will show the Higgs mechanism generates the mass in the scaling limits in Theorem 3.1
and Theorem 3.2.
    Proving mass gap in Yang–Mills theories is a fundamental open question in d ≥ 3.
For pure gauge theories on the lattice (i.e., no Higgs field and no scaling limit), mass gap
was rigorously proved at strong coupling (large g) by Osterwalder and Seiler [38] using
the cluster expansion technique of Glimm, Jaffe, and Spencer [29]. Similar arguments
were used by Seiler [39] to prove mass gap in Yang–Mills–Higgs models on lattices when
αg is large enough. An important recent work on mass gap is the paper by Shen, Zhu,
and Zhu [42], which improves the large g condition in [38]. In particular, [42] allows g
to be chosen uniformly in N for SU(N ) theory.
    The problem with holding αg larger than a constant, however, is that the correlation
length does not diverge to infinity [39] and therefore one cannot pass to a continuum
limit. In this sense, Theorem 3.2 is the first rigorous proof of the validity of the Higgs
mechanism in the continuum limit of a Euclidean Yang–Mills theory in a dimension
bigger than two. It also gives a partial explanation for the phase diagrams constructed
by Fradkin and Shenker [25], Banks and Rabinovici [9] and many subsequent authors in
the physics literature, which suggest that a mass gap can be present even if αg is very
small.
    In Fradkin and Shenker [25, Fig. 2], for instance, the phase diagram for the U(1)
model on the lattice (without taking a scaling limit) indicates that correlations decay
exponentially when either g is bigger than some threshold g0 (which is already provable
rigorously from the results of [38]), or if α2 is bigger than some concave function of 1/g 2
growing slower than linearly. In particular, this phase diagram (if true) implies that the
region of exponential decay of correlations contains values of (α, g) such that the product
αg is smaller than any given constant. This goes beyond the ‘strong coupling’ region
given by Seiler [39] where αg is bigger than a fixed constant. It is difficult, however,
to compare such phase diagrams with our results, since we obtain exponential decay of
correlations only in the continuum scaling limit. But we do take αg to zero while taking
this limit, which gives partial support for the predictions from the physics papers.

3.5   Open questions
It is evident from the discussion in the previous subsection that holding αg = cε is the
key fact that gives rise to a massive field in the scaling limit. It is natural to ask whether
sending g to zero so fast that g = O(ε50d ) is really necessary for obtaining this limit.
The answer may depend on the model. For U(1) theory, it is possible that simply under
the conditions that αg = cε and g → 0 as ε → 0, with no further conditions on the
decay rate of g (or perhaps a very slow decay rate), we obtain the scaling limit given
by Theorem 3.1. For SU(2) theory, it seems likely that a slow decay of g will cause
non-Abelian effects to manifest themselves, possibly leading to a different scaling limit.
It would be interesting to figure out the various regimes for both theories. But holding
αg = cε seems to be a necessary condition for mass generation in all cases.
    Another open problem is to extend the results to general Higgs potentials, going
beyond the degenerate potential considered in this paper. It is not clear how to make
this extension.
